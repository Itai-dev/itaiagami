What museums get wrong about technology

Technology fails in museums when it becomes the exhibit. It works when it disappears into the story. Notes from years of designing interactive exhibitions for cultural institutions.

Every museum director has seen it: the expensive touchscreen standing next to a four-hundred-year-old artifact, ignored by everyone over twelve and mashed like a vending machine by everyone under it. The institution bought “innovation.” The visitors got a kiosk.

The mistake is almost never the technology itself. Projection, tracking, real-time rendering, touch — all of it works, and all of it has earned its place in exhibition design. The mistake is the question the technology was bought to answer. “How do we look modern?” produces kiosks. “How do we make this story land?” produces experiences.

The screen is a rival

A museum is one of the last places where people stand in front of real things. That is its entire advantage — the artifact, the room, the presence. A screen next to an artifact is not a supplement; it is a rival, and a strong one, because screens are what our hands already know. Put a monitor beside a mosaic and watch where the eyes go.

The technology that works in exhibitions refuses to compete with the artifact. It wraps around the story instead: projection that lives on the architecture itself, sound that guides you through a space, interfaces that ask for your body — a hand, a step, a shadow — rather than your screen habits. When it’s done well, visitors afterwards describe what they experienced, not what they operated.

Nobody leaves a great exhibition saying “the touchscreens were excellent.” They say: I walked through Jerusalem’s history. I made the forest bloom. I heard the letters read aloud.

Design for ninety seconds and two pulled hands

Institutions brief for an imagined visitor who reads every label. The real visitor is a parent with a child pulling at each hand and ninety seconds of attention, a teenager who has never voluntarily read wall text, a tourist with no common language with your curators. This is not the audience problem — it is the design brief.

Interaction earns its place when it meets that reality: understandable in three seconds, rewarding in ten, deep enough to hold whoever stays for five minutes. If it needs instructions, it has already failed. The best compliment an interactive exhibit can receive is a queue of children who understood it before their parents finished reading the sign.

The curator is not the obstacle

There is a quiet war in many institutions between the people who hold the knowledge and the people who bring the technology, and most bad museum tech is a monument to that war ending badly — either scholarship with a screen bolted on, or spectacle with a fact sheet attached.

The projects that work are the ones where the creative team treats curators as co-authors, not clients to be managed. The scholarship is the material. Technology is a way of staging it. When an environmental scientist shapes what an interactive forest teaches, or a historian decides what a projected city must get right, the result holds up to both audiences it has to serve: the expert who checks, and the child who touches.

Three questions before buying anything

  1. What should a visitor feel in this room? If the answer is a fact, write a label. Technology is for feelings — scale, wonder, being inside.
  2. What does it replace? If the honest answer is “nothing — it’s additional,” it will be additional forever: additional maintenance, additional noise, additional thing to walk past.
  3. Will it still matter switched off? The strongest experiences use technology to reveal the collection, so even the moment the projector rests, the story it told keeps working in the visitor’s memory. If the room is empty without the screens, the room was empty with them.

Museums don’t need to look modern. They hold the oldest advantage there is: real things, real rooms, real presence. Technology earns its place the moment it serves that — and not a moment before.
