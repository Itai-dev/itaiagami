How to brief a creative director

Most briefs describe a deliverable. The useful ones describe a problem. Here is what I actually need to know before the first idea, and what you can safely leave out.

The quality of creative work is decided earlier than most people think. Not in the design reviews, not in the production. In the brief. A sharp brief makes good work likely. A vague one guarantees rounds of revisions that are really just the brief being written after the fact, expensively.

After years of receiving briefs from technology companies, broadcasters, museums and agencies, I can say the pattern clearly: the weakest briefs describe a deliverable; the strongest describe a problem. “We need a launch video” is an order. “Nobody understands why our product matters, and we have one moment to change that” is a brief. The second one gives a creative director something to solve.

What I actually need to know

The problem, in one honest sentence. Not the polished version for the board. The real one. “People try the product and don’t come back.” “The institution feels old to people under forty.” “We do serious work and look like a startup template.” Honesty here saves weeks.

Who has to feel something. Not demographics: situations. “A museum visitor with kids pulling at both hands and ninety seconds of attention.” “A CTO who has seen a hundred vendor decks this quarter.” I can design for that person. I cannot design for “25–54, urban, digitally savvy.”

What success looks like, concretely. If this works, what is different a year later? More visitors? A story the press retells? A sales team that finally has an answer to “what do you do”? The measure of success shapes the work more than any aesthetic preference.

The real constraints. Budget range, hard deadlines, technical realities, politics. Constraints are not obstacles to the creative work. They are the walls the idea bounces off. The worst constraint is the one revealed in round three.

What you would never do. Taste is easier to express in negatives, and more reliable. “Nothing corporate-blue.” “No stock people laughing at laptops.” “We never want to look clever at the audience’s expense.” Three sentences of this beat a fifty-page brand book.

What you can leave out

The solution - unless you already have one. If the idea is set, say so plainly and share the direction, the storyboard or the director’s treatment; the brief then becomes about developing and executing it well, which is work I do too. What doesn’t help is a half-decided solution disguised as a problem. The references worth sending are the ones that show how something felt, not how it looked.

You can also leave out perfection. A brief with honest gaps (“we don’t know our audience as well as we should”) is more useful than one with confident guesses. Gaps are findable. Wrong certainties get built on.

A brief is not a specification. It is the first creative act of the project: deciding, precisely, what problem is worth solving.

A short template

  1. Problem: one honest sentence.
  2. Audience: one person, in one situation.
  3. Success: what is different a year later.
  4. Constraints: budget range, dates, realities.
  5. Never: three things you would not accept.

One page. If it takes five, the problem isn’t defined yet, which is also useful to know, because defining it is work I can help with. Some of the best projects I’ve been part of started exactly there: not with a brief, but with the conversation that produced one.
